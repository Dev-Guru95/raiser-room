# Where the valuation orientation text comes from

The valuation-readiness feature makes almost no claims about the world. The few
descriptive statements it does make — what the two common kinds of valuation are
for, and why an appraisal of ordinary shares commonly lands below the price paid
for preferred shares — are recorded here with their sources, because the product
holds external claims to a standard (publisher, title, date, access date) and
should not exempt its own copy from it.

## What is asserted, and on what basis

**That a fundraising valuation is negotiated with investors and is typically a
price for preferred shares, while an independent appraisal is a formal opinion of
fair market value produced by an independent appraiser.**

> "an independent appraisal of a private company's fair market value (FMV)" …
> "negotiated with investors, typically for preferred shares"

— Burkland Associates, *Avoiding Startup Valuation Traps: 409A vs. Fundraising*,
22 April 2025. <https://burklandassociates.com/2025/04/22/avoiding-startup-valuation-traps-409a-vs-fundraising/>
Accessed 13 October 2026.

**That an appraisal of ordinary shares commonly lands below the preferred price,
because preferred shares carry rights ordinary shares do not and because a
private company's shares cannot readily be sold.**

> "The common stock lacks certain investor protections that are granted to
> preferred shares" … appraisals "apply discounts to account for illiquidity and
> lack of marketability"

— same source, same access date.

## What is deliberately NOT asserted

**No tax or regulatory rule.** Not when a valuation must be refreshed, not how
long one lasts, not what happens if one is wrong, not what a safe harbour is or
requires. These are jurisdiction-specific and they change.

The primary source for the United States position is the Internal Revenue Code
section 409A and the regulations under it; IRS Notice 2005-1 is interim guidance
and does **not** contain the safe-harbour presumption that secondary sources
commonly describe, which is in the final regulations. That gap between what
secondary sources say and what the primary source actually contains is precisely
why the product stays out of it: a summary that is subtly wrong is worse than no
summary, because a founder relies on it and then has a tax problem and a
screenshot.

The product therefore says what the two kinds of valuation are *for*, so a
founder knows which conversation they are having, and points at the professionals
who have those conversations.

**No figure of any kind.** No typical multiple, no range, no discount, no
benchmark. Nothing in the valuation feature is measured.

**No vendor, product or firm name.** The product names none anywhere, and this
feature is the one where naming one would matter most: a recommendation issued by
software sold by a party connected to a firm that then advises on the transaction
is worth less than no recommendation at all.

One practical illustration of why vendor names do not belong in a product:
while researching this feature on 13 October 2026, a widely used cap-table and
valuation provider's own product page carried a notice that the company is
shutting down on 8 December 2026. A vendor name written into the product in
October would have been pointing founders at a closed service by Christmas.
